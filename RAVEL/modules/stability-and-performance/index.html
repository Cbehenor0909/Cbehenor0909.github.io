State what stability means

Read the diagram. Horizontal position gives exponential decay or growth; vertical position gives oscillation frequency. The highlighted pair for the later worked example oscillates while its envelope decays. An imaginary-axis mode has no exponential decay; repeated defective modes still need a separate stability check. These are pole locations, rather than positions traveled by the physical system.

For an autonomous continuous-time LTI state model, asymptotic internal stability means every initial state decays to zero; equivalently, AAA is Hurwitz. Lyapunov stability only requires sufficiently small initial perturbations to remain small. Semisimple imaginary-axis modes can meet the latter without decaying.

BIBO stability means every bounded input gives a bounded zero-state output. For a proper rational transfer function, all its reduced poles must lie strictly in the left half-plane. A constant feedthrough term is compatible with BIBO stability. Hidden unstable states can make a nonminimal realization internally unstable even when its input/output map is BIBO stable.

A lossless oscillator is Lyapunov stable but is not BIBO stable: bounded resonant forcing produces growing amplitude. State which definition is being used before describing a loop as stable.

The explorer’s K=0K=0K=0 case makes the distinction concrete. The unreduced characteristic polynomial still has a zero root, and an initial displacement persists, so the three-state realization is not asymptotically internally stable. Its zero-state reference-to-output map reduces to T(s)=0T(s)=0T(s)=0, which is BIBO stable: every bounded reference produces zero output from rest. A zero step trace therefore cannot establish internal asymptotic stability. At K=6K=6K=6, even a bounded step response does not establish BIBO stability; resonant forcing can excite the imaginary-axis poles without bound.

Steady-state error and sensitivity

For negative unity feedback with plant PPP, controller CCC and L=PCL=PCL=PC,

S=11+L,T=L1+L,S+T=1.S=\frac{1}{1+L},\qquad T=\frac{L}{1+L},\qquad S+T=1.S=1+L1​,T=1+LL​,S+T=1.

If an output disturbance ddd adds to the plant output and sensor noise nnn adds to the measured output, then

y=Tr+Sd−Tn.y=Tr+Sd-Tn.y=Tr+Sd−Tn.

Thus large low-frequency loop gain reduces reference error and output disturbances, while large high-frequency TTT transmits sensor noise. For a disturbance added at the plant input, the output map is PSPSPS, not SSS. Injection location must accompany every sensitivity claim. The frequency-design chapter discusses these tradeoffs.

Oral practice

Continue to the Root Locus explorer to reveal the complete gain-indexed paths behind the selected poles shown here. Locate the breakaway and imaginary-axis crossing, then test whether a desired pole can be reached by a positive scalar gain.

  • Can all polynomial coefficients be positive in an unstable system? Yes. For the cubic above, K>6K>6K>6 is a counterexample.
  • Does pole-zero cancellation establish internal stability? No. It may hide an unstable mode.
  • Can sensitivity and complementary sensitivity both be tiny at one frequency? Their complex sum is one, so they cannot both have arbitrarily small magnitude there.

Use root locus to see the gain-dependent pole motion, and Bode loop shaping to balance tracking, robustness and noise.

  • Compute second-order response metrics with explicit assumptions.
  • Interpret sensitivity and steady-state tracking errors.
