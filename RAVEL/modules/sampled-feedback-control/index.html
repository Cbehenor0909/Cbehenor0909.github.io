Sampled Feedback · Timing, Noise and Limits

Follow a measurement through sampling, velocity estimation, digital feedback and held actuator effort, and see why delay and saturation change the closed loop.

intermediateFunctionalReading lesson

What happens between a sensor timestamp and the force that finally reaches the mechanism?

A feedback loop has a clock

The mechanism moves continuously while the controller sees selected snapshots. A sampled sensor value describes the past by the time estimation, transport and computation finish. An actuator typically holds its latest command until the next update. These timing operations belong to the loop model.

Read the diagram. Teal identifies the measurement path, blue the estimated state and computation, and red the applied force. Sampling turns a continuous signal into a sequence. The hold turns commands back into a piecewise-constant input. Delay can occur at several locations; its location and timestamp convention must be stated.

Noise, aliasing and delay are different

Differencing amplifies position noise by a factor related to 1/Ts1/T_s1/Ts​. Faster sampling does not automatically improve a noisy derivative. An analog anti-alias filter acts before sampling; a digital filter cannot undo frequencies already folded into the sampled band. The band-limited sampling theorem is a reconstruction condition, not a closed-loop performance guarantee.

A pure delay contributes phase −ωτ-\omega\tau−ωτ radians. Increasing gain raises effort demands; extra delay and filtering can reduce robustness. If integral feedback is added, use a defined anti-windup strategy when commands saturate. See Bode Loop Shaping for the frequency viewpoint.

Oral checks and next experiment

  • Does a smooth measured trace imply small delay? No. Filtering can make it smooth while increasing lag.
  • Does a saturation clamp preserve the linear poles? No. The closed loop becomes nonlinear when the clamp is active.
  • What must a timestamp identify? When the measurement was acquired, together with its clock and relation to actuation time.

Common error: choosing gains from a continuous model and treating the computer as instantaneous. In the Sensing-to-Control Lab, compare 50, 100 and 200 Hz updates, delayed measurements, deterministic position noise and a 40 N40\,\mathrm N40N force bound on the same synthetic cart. Its results illustrate a declared model rather than device performance.

  • Calculate a filtered finite-difference velocity and bounded PD command.
  • Derive the exact stability range of a sampled scalar integrator.
  • Distinguish aliasing, differentiation noise, latency and saturation.
