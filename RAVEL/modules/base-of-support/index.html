Intuition

The base of support is the region enclosed by the body’s contacts with a supporting surface. In this simplified planar model, it is the convex hull of the active foot contact patches. With both feet on the ground, the region includes the space between them. When one foot leaves the ground, only the remaining contact patch contributes.

Math

Let ClC_lCl​ and CrC_rCr​ denote the left and right contact patches. In double support,

BOS=conv⁡(Cl∪Cr).\mathrm{BOS} = \operatorname{conv}(C_l\cup C_r).BOS=conv(Cl​∪Cr​).

In left single support,

BOS=conv⁡(Cl).\mathrm{BOS} = \operatorname{conv}(C_l).BOS=conv(Cl​).

Here, conv⁡\operatorname{conv}conv means the smallest convex region containing the contacts.

Assumptions

  • Flat, rigid ground; two coplanar, full-foot contact patches.
  • Fixed, dimensionless footprint geometry viewed from above; not to scale.
  • Prescribed contact timing: double support at 0–10% and 50–60%, left single support at 10–50%, and right single support at 60–100%.
  • Phase 100% equals the start of the next cycle. Timing is illustrative, not subject-specific.
  • Contact changes instantaneously. Heel/toe roll, sliding, foot travel, forces, and body dynamics are omitted.

Common misconception

A larger support region alone does not guarantee balance. This diagram describes contact geometry; it cannot determine whether a moving person will recover from a disturbance.

Connection

The same support-polygon idea appears in human stance and legged robots. Future modules can connect contact geometry to body motion and forces after introducing the necessary assumptions.

Check your understanding

  • Identify the contacts that contribute to the base of support.
  • Explain why the region between two contacting feet belongs to the support polygon.
  • Distinguish support geometry from a prediction of dynamic balance.

References
