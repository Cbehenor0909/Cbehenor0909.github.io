Three things to notice

First, the same motion has different descriptions for different observers. Frame BBB is attached to the turntable. The bug moves along a straight radial slot at constant speed uuu, so its relative acceleration is Barel=0{}^{B}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{rel}} = \mathbf{0}Barel​=0. A ground observer in NNN sees an outward spiral when Ω>0\Omega > 0Ω>0 and u>0u > 0u>0, with centripetal and Coriolis contributions to inertial acceleration. This illustrates the difference between B ⁣ddt{}^{B}\!\frac{d}{dt}Bdtd​ and N ⁣ddt{}^{N}\!\frac{d}{dt}Ndtd​. When u=0u = 0u=0, the inertial path is a circle; when Ω=0\Omega = 0Ω=0, it is a straight line. The zero in the right panel is relative acceleration: expressing inertial acceleration in the BBB basis does not make it zero.

Second, watch the dashed axes (n^x,n^y)(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_x,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_y)(n^x​,n^y​) in the right panel. An observer on the turntable sees the ground basis rotate in the opposite direction. Angular velocity is relative: ωN/B=−ωB/N\boldsymbol{\omega}^{N/B} = -\boldsymbol{\omega}^{B/N}ωN/B=−ωB/N. This equation compares geometric vectors. To compare their components, first express both vectors in the same basis.

Third, set Ω\OmegaΩ to zero. With the two bases initially aligned, the trajectories and acceleration descriptions agree, and both accelerations vanish in this example. This is a useful transport-theorem check: when BBB does not rotate, the two observer derivatives agree. Next, increase uuu at t=0t = 0t=0: the purple Coriolis arrow grows while the green centripetal arrow stays unchanged. The centripetal term depends on Ω2r\Omega^2rΩ2r; it is independent of uuu only when Ω\OmegaΩ and rrr are fixed. During playback, a larger uuu makes rrr grow faster, so the centripetal term also grows faster. Finally, set u=0u = 0u=0: Coriolis acceleration vanishes, but centripetal acceleration may remain.

Transport theorem and the five-term formula

For any vector A\mathbf{A}A, with ω=ωB/N\boldsymbol{\omega} = \boldsymbol{\omega}^{B/N}ω=ωB/N:

N ⁣dAdt=B ⁣dAdt+ω×A.{}^{N}\!\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt} = {}^{B}\!\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt} + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{A}.NdtdA​=BdtdA​+ω×A.

The superscript identifies the observer derivative, rather than merely the basis used for components. Let ρ\boldsymbol{\rho}ρ be the position of PPP relative to the origin OOO of frame BBB, with

Bvrel=B ⁣dρdt,Barel=B ⁣d2ρdt2,α=N ⁣dωdt.\begin{aligned} {}^{B}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rel}} &= {}^{B}\!\frac{d\boldsymbol{\rho}}{dt}, \\ {}^{B}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{rel}} &= {}^{B}\!\frac{d^2\boldsymbol{\rho}}{dt^2}, \\ \boldsymbol{\alpha} &= {}^{N}\!\frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{dt}. \end{aligned}Bvrel​Barel​α​=Bdtdρ​,=Bdt2d2ρ​,=Ndtdω​.​

Then

NaP=NaO+Barel+2ω×Bvrel+α×ρ+ω×(ω×ρ).\begin{aligned} {}^{N}\mathbf{a}^{P} &= {}^{N}\mathbf{a}^{O} + {}^{B}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{rel}} + 2\boldsymbol{\omega}\times{}^{B}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rel}} \\ &\quad + \boldsymbol{\alpha}\times\boldsymbol{\rho} + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\rho}). \end{aligned}NaP​=NaO+Barel​+2ω×Bvrel​+α×ρ+ω×(ω×ρ).​

Express all terms in the same basis before adding them. This is a kinematic relation. The relative motion is prescribed here; the demonstration does not solve for motion from forces.

Why is Coriolis acceleration 2Ωu?

Start with NvP=ub^x+Ωrb^y{}^{N}\mathbf{v}^{P} = u\hat{\mathbf{b}}_x + \Omega r\hat{\mathbf{b}}_yNvP=ub^x​+Ωrb^y​. Over a short interval dtdtdt, two changes contribute to the transverse velocity:

  1. The relative velocity rotates with the frame. The direction of ub^xu\hat{\mathbf{b}}_xub^x​ changes with the turntable, contributing Ωu dt b^y\Omega u\,dt\,\hat{\mathbf{b}}_yΩudtb^y​. This comes from applying the transport theorem to Bvrel{}^{B}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rel}}Bvrel​.
  2. The transport velocity grows as the bug moves outward. The bug moves outward by u dtu\,dtudt, increasing Ωr\Omega rΩr by Ωu dt\Omega u\,dtΩudt. This comes from the change of ρ\boldsymbol{\rho}ρ within BBB when differentiating ω×ρ\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\rho}ω×ρ.

The contributions have the same direction and magnitude, giving 2Ωub^y2\Omega u\hat{\mathbf{b}}_y2Ωub^y​. The changing direction of the transport velocity separately contributes −Ω2rb^x-\Omega^2r\hat{\mathbf{b}}_x−Ω2rb^x​, the centripetal term.

Practice and assumptions

Predict how the arrows will change before adjusting Ω\OmegaΩ and uuu. Compare parameters at a fixed radius; the controls reset to r0r_0r0​ when Ω\OmegaΩ or uuu changes. Practice explaining Example 1 aloud, then consider a variant with α≠0\boldsymbol{\alpha} \neq \mathbf{0}α=0: the five-term formula acquires a tangential contribution α×ρ\boldsymbol{\alpha}\times\boldsymbol{\rho}α×ρ. That variant is a practice question; the current interaction keeps α=0\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathbf{0}α=0.

  • Fixed, coincident origins; uniform planar rigid-body rotation; the bases are aligned at t=0t = 0t=0.
  • The radial speed is prescribed and constant. Forces, friction, and dynamic equations are omitted.
  • Changing Ω\OmegaΩ or uuu pauses and resets the motion. The drag history is not an angular-acceleration experiment.
  • Playback has a finite endpoint, with no automatic start or loop. The initial diagram and explanation remain available without JavaScript.
  • This is an analytic, conceptual kinematics demonstration. It has not been validated with hardware, experiments, or teaching outcomes.

Check your understanding

  • Distinguish an observer derivative from a vector's components in a chosen basis.
  • Apply the five-term acceleration formula and explain the factor of two in Coriolis acceleration.
  • Check the limiting cases of zero spin and zero relative speed.
