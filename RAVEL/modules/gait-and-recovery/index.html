Gait and Recovery · Contacts, Steps and Timing

Track loaded support through a walking cycle, distinguish future foot placement from current contact, and calculate how recovery timing changes a model capture target.

intermediateFunctionalReading lesson

How do current support, future foot placement and the time to touchdown jointly affect recovery?

Support changes before and after a step

Walking repeatedly changes which contact can supply ground force. During double support, both feet are loaded. During single support, one foot supports the body while the other swings. A foot drawn near the floor is not automatically loaded support, and a planned landing location supplies no ground reaction force before touchdown.

Read the diagram. Solid outlines identify the active feet used to construct the blue support hull. A dashed outline is explicitly labeled swing and is excluded. The third panel introduces the new contact only after touchdown. This is a schematic walking sequence, not a measured gait cycle or a prescription for phase durations.

The flat-ground support polygon is the convex hull of active contact patches. It describes feasible geometry under stated contact assumptions. Heel-only or toe-only contact can shrink the patch; friction and normal-load distribution can further restrict usable forces. A handrail contact also changes the mechanics, but a horizontal foot polygon alone does not describe that three-dimensional situation.

Moving COP within a loaded foot changes COM acceleration. A new step changes where contact can act after landing. Trunk and arm motion can contribute through centroidal angular momentum, a mechanism excluded from this simple LIPM. Hof’s walking study motivates considering velocity and lateral foot placement; Koolen and colleagues define capturability with a limited number of steps and specified models.

Feasible recovery needs more than a target

Check whether the swing foot can reach the required placement in the available time, avoid obstacles and collisions, establish a nonslipping contact, and produce the required wrench. Joint ranges, torque limits and existing momentum matter. Multiple steps can recover states outside a one-step capture region. Conversely, a geometric landing target can be unreachable. See Robot Dynamics and Actuation for force capacity and Sampled Feedback Control for delay.

Contact event detection also needs a declared load threshold, synchronized signals and inspection of missing or ambiguous samples. Human Motion Sensing explains the measurement chain. These models support explanation and controller reasoning; they do not establish a person’s fall risk, rehabilitation outcome or a clinically valid recovery threshold.

Common mistakes

  • Including the airborne foot in the current support polygon.
  • Treating a planned footstep as an instantaneous COP command.
  • Ignoring touchdown delay when computing a capture location.
  • Calling every state outside the current polygon unrecoverable.

Oral practice

  1. Explain how a contact polygon changes at lift-off and touchdown.
  2. Calculate how a longer delay shifts a fixed-COP capture target.
  3. Name three reasons a mathematically computed step could be infeasible.

Check your understanding

  • Identify single and double support from loaded contact.
  • Distinguish a swing foot from an active support region.
  • Compute a touchdown capture target with a specified delay.
  • Explain reachability and model limitations in recovery planning.
