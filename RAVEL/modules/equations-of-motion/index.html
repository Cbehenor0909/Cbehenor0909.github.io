Deriving and Integrating Equations of Motion

Derive a mechanical model using Lagrange's equations, compare Newton–Euler and D'Alembert formulations, and check numerical integration against physics.

advancedFunctionalReading lesson

How do physical assumptions become differential equations, and how do we know a numerical trajectory solves those equations accurately?

Three routes to the same mechanics

Newton–Euler writes force and moment balances on each particle/body. It exposes joint reactions and is useful when those loads matter. D’Alembert projects force-minus-inertial terms onto admissible virtual displacements. Lagrange organizes that projection using generalized coordinates and kinetic/potential energy. Their predictions agree when the bodies, constraints, loads and conventions agree.

Redundant coordinates can retain constraints through Lagrange multipliers; nonholonomic constraints require an appropriate constrained formulation rather than careless substitution into this minimal-coordinate equation.

The mass matrix couples accelerations; c\mathbf cc contains velocity-dependent effects. Do not assume it is a unique named matrix without defining the chosen representation. Solve the linear system for accelerations rather than explicitly forming M−1M^{-1}M−1 in an implementation. Contact and redundant-coordinate constraints can turn the problem into a constrained differential-algebraic system.

Oral checks and common error

Common error: presenting a plausible animation as evidence that an equation or integration is correct.

  • Why can ideal pin reactions disappear in Lagrange’s equation? Their virtual work on admissible motion is zero; their physical forces still exist.
  • Which method is best when joint reactions are required? Newton–Euler or a constrained formulation that retains/reconstructs reactions.
  • What distinguishes accuracy from stability? Accuracy bounds solution error; stability controls how numerical errors propagate. A stable result can still be inaccurate.

The MIT Lagrange notes develop the energy formulation; the MIT numerical-method material provides the RK4 algorithm.

Check your understanding

  • Derive a complete rigid-pendulum equation from kinetic and potential energy.
  • Explain how force, virtual-work and energy formulations describe the same mechanics.
  • Convert second-order dynamics to a first-order initial-value problem and assess integration error.
