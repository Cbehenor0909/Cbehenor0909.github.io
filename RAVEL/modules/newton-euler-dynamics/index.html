Newton–Euler Dynamics and Inertial Frames

Relate external forces and moments to motion for particles, rigid bodies and interconnected systems, with explicit inertial-frame assumptions.

advancedFunctionalReading lesson

Which external forces and moments are required to produce a specified translational and rotational acceleration?

Choose an inertial observer

Newton’s first law characterizes an inertial frame: a free particle has constant velocity there. For a constant-mass particle observed in such a frame,

∑F=ma=Ndpdt,p=mv.\sum\mathbf F=m\mathbf a=\frac{{}^{N}d\mathbf p}{dt},\qquad \mathbf p=m\mathbf v.∑F=ma=dtNdp​,p=mv.

The derivative belongs to the inertial observer NNN. A rotating basis can still express this equation, provided inertial acceleration is computed correctly. A rotating observer’s relative acceleration alone is insufficient. Translating/rotating noninertial descriptions require the corresponding acceleration terms, sometimes moved to the force side as fictitious forces. The Transport Formula supplies those kinematics.

Newton’s third law gives equal and opposite interaction forces between two bodies. These forces cancel in their combined linear momentum balance. Under the classical mechanical model, internal moments also cancel when the interaction is represented consistently by forces at the same interface and opposite couples, or by central particle force pairs. Keep every reaction when analyzing one body alone.

Rigid-body translation and rotation

For a rigid body of constant mass with COM CCC,

∑Fext=MaC,∑τC,ext=N ⁣dHCdt.\sum\mathbf F_{\mathrm{ext}}=M\mathbf a_C,\qquad \sum\boldsymbol\tau_{C,\mathrm{ext}}={}^{N}\!\frac{d\mathbf H_C}{dt}.∑Fext​=MaC​,∑τC,ext​=NdtdHC​​.

Use HC=ICω\mathbf H_C=\mathsf I_C\boldsymbol\omegaHC​=IC​ω. In a body-fixed basis, the inertia components are constant, and transport gives

[τC]B=ICB[α]B+[ω]B×(ICB[ω]B).[\boldsymbol\tau_C]_B =I_C^B[\boldsymbol\alpha]_B +[\boldsymbol\omega]_B\times\left(I_C^B[\boldsymbol\omega]_B\right).[τC​]B​=ICB​[α]B​+[ω]B​×(ICB​[ω]B​).

The second term is gyroscopic. It accounts for the changing direction of angular momentum as the body basis turns. Since ω×ω=0\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf0ω×ω=0, differentiating the body’s own angular-velocity components gives angular-acceleration components; this special fact does not hold for arbitrary vectors.

About principal axes, the first Euler equation is τ1=I1ω˙1+(I3−I2)ω2ω3\tau_1=I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3τ1​=I1​ω˙1​+(I3​−I2​)ω2​ω3​, with cyclic analogues. In planar rotation about a centroidal principal axis, the gyroscopic term vanishes and τC,z=IC,zzαz\tau_{C,z}=I_{C,zz}\alpha_zτC,z​=IC,zz​αz​. The scalar relation is a special case, not the general spatial rule. Moments about an arbitrary moving point need additional care; do not substitute that point’s inertia into the centroidal equation automatically.

Multibody equations and D’Alembert’s principle

For a linkage, write force and moment balances for each link, then use the joint kinematics and reaction pairs to couple the equations. Forward dynamics finds accelerations from loads and state. Inverse dynamics finds required generalized loads from a specified motion; uniqueness and feasibility depend on actuation, constraints and contact assumptions.

D’Alembert’s principle rewrites particle dynamics using admissible virtual displacements at a fixed time:

∑i(Fi−miai)⋅δri=0.\sum_i(\mathbf F_i-m_i\mathbf a_i)\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0.i∑​(Fi​−mi​ai​)⋅δri​=0.

Ideal constraint forces have zero virtual work and can disappear after projection onto independent coordinates. The term −miai-m_i\mathbf a_i−mi​ai​ is an inertial term in the formulation, not an extra physical force in the inertial free-body diagram. For rigid bodies the corresponding inertial moment is −NdHC/dt-{}^Nd\mathbf H_C/dt−NdHC​/dt, including the gyroscopic contribution. This route connects Newton–Euler balances to Lagrange’s equations.

Oral checks and common error

Common error: using F=marel\mathbf F=m\mathbf a_{\mathrm{rel}}F=marel​ in a rotating frame without the transport terms, or deleting joint reactions from each isolated link.

  • Can COM acceleration be found without solving every internal force? Yes: sum external forces and divide by total constant mass.
  • When is τ=Iα\boldsymbol\tau=I\boldsymbol\alphaτ=Iα sufficient? Under the relevant scalar planar/principal-axis assumptions; otherwise retain tensor and gyroscopic terms.
  • Does D’Alembert mean the moving system is physically static? No. It is a rearrangement/projection of the dynamic equations.

The MIT robotics dynamics chapter develops individual-link balances and their reduction to joint equations.

Check your understanding

  • Apply linear and angular momentum balance with the correct observer derivative.
  • Explain internal action–reaction cancellation and individual-body reactions.
  • Recognize the gyroscopic term and the role of ideal constraints in multibody dynamics.
