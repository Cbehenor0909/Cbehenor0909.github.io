Sensor-to-Segment Calibration · Separate Mounting from Motion

Define a sensor mounting transform, recover segment orientation, and distinguish an anatomical frame from a convenient sensor frame.

intermediateFunctionalReading lesson

Which part of an IMU orientation belongs to the segment and which part belongs to its attachment?

One attachment, two kinds of rotation

An IMU can be attached obliquely while the arm is straight. Its orientation therefore contains both segment motion and a mounting offset. Calibrating that offset lets the same segment motion produce the same reconstructed segment orientation after different attachments, provided the calibration assumptions hold.

Read the diagram. The dark segment and small sensor rotate together. The blue sensor axis remains offset from the ochre segment axis in both poses. The bottom arrows map coordinates; they are not physical movements. A mounting calibration identifies the first mapping, while an orientation estimate supplies the complete sensor-to-world mapping.

Here NNN is a fixed world frame, BBB a segment frame and SSS the attached sensor frame. As in IMU Coordinate Frames, column-vector components obey RABvB=vAR_{AB}\mathbf{v}_B=\mathbf{v}_ARAB​vB​=vA​. Thus

RNS=RNBRBS,RNB=RNSRBST.R_{NS}=R_{NB}R_{BS},\qquad R_{NB}=R_{NS}R_{BS}^{\mathsf T}.RNS​=RNB​RBS​,RNB​=RNS​RBST​.

RBSR_{BS}RBS​ maps sensor components into segment components; its transpose RSBR_{SB}RSB​ maps in the opposite direction. Calling either one an “offset” without its direction makes the multiplication ambiguous. In general, rotations cannot be reordered.

What establishes the segment frame?

An anatomical frame needs defined landmarks or a stated functional construction, origin, axis directions and handedness. Simply renaming the sensor’s axes “flexion” and “abduction” does not establish that frame. ISB recommendations provide joint-specific reporting conventions; this schematic does not implement a clinical joint coordinate system.

In a pose-based calibration, assume that the segment’s orientation RNBcalR_{NB}^{\mathrm{cal}}RNBcal​ is known and that the sensor estimate RNScalR_{NS}^{\mathrm{cal}}RNScal​ uses the same world frame. Then

RBS=(RNBcal)TRNScal.R_{BS}=(R_{NB}^{\mathrm{cal}})^{\mathsf T}R_{NS}^{\mathrm{cal}}.RBS​=(RNBcal​)TRNScal​.

OpenSim IMU Placer uses a calibration pose matching the model’s default pose to determine sensor-frame offsets and can apply a heading adjustment. A mistaken pose can be absorbed into the estimated offset rather than revealed by it.

Functional calibration instead uses prescribed motions and a model to identify axes. Its result depends on whether those motions sufficiently excite the intended degrees of freedom and satisfy the model. Skin motion, attachment slip and coupled joint motion can violate a rigid-axis assumption.

Worked mounting correction

Consider an ideal planar model, with all rotations about the shared positive zzz axis. During calibration the segment is aligned with NNN, but the sensor is mounted 90∘90^{\circ}90∘ counterclockwise:

RNBcal=I,RNScal=Rz(90∘),RBS=Rz(90∘).R_{NB}^{\mathrm{cal}}=I,\qquad R_{NS}^{\mathrm{cal}}=R_z(90^{\circ}),\qquad R_{BS}=R_z(90^{\circ}).RNBcal​=I,RNScal​=Rz​(90∘),RBS​=Rz​(90∘).

Later the sensor estimate is Rz(120∘)R_z(120^{\circ})Rz​(120∘). Correcting the mounting gives

RNB=Rz(120∘)Rz(−90∘)=Rz(30∘).R_{NB}=R_z(120^{\circ})R_z(-90^{\circ}) =R_z(30^{\circ}).RNB​=Rz​(120∘)Rz​(−90∘)=Rz​(30∘).

The segment moved 30∘30^{\circ}30∘, not 120∘120^{\circ}120∘. In the mounting transform, a vector [1,0,0]T[1,0,0]^{\mathsf T}[1,0,0]T along the sensor’s xxx axis becomes [0,1,0]T[0,1,0]^{\mathsf T}[0,1,0]T in BBB. Subtracting scalar angles works in this deliberately shared-axis example; the matrix composition is the general rule.

Separate three calibration questions

Sensor calibration addresses quantities such as scale and bias. Sensor-to-segment calibration addresses attachment geometry. Heading registration aligns the chosen world direction. Solving one does not automatically solve the other two.

A stationary accelerometer constrains the direction of gravity in the sensor frame. Rotation about gravity leaves that direction unchanged, so gravity alone cannot establish heading. A known full pose therefore includes information beyond a static accelerometer reading. Consistent world headings are particularly important when combining independently estimated segments.

The fixed-offset relation assumes rigid attachment, synchronized pose information, correct units and compatible orientation conventions. Reattachment changes RBSR_{BS}RBS​. A smoother filter cannot restore a changed attachment transform.

What a calibration review adds

Pacher et al. (2020) systematically review lower-body sensor-to-segment calibration. They distinguish manual, static, functional and anatomical methods, including combinations. Their heterogeneous evaluation protocols make a universal ranking difficult. Use the review to ask which axes each procedure identifies and how it was evaluated, rather than choosing a method from a single accuracy number. Its lower-body scope does not automatically establish upper-limb performance.

The paper guide connects this review to orientation estimation and model-based inverse kinematics.

Common errors

  • Multiply by RBSR_{BS}RBS​ when the recovery requires RSB=RBSTR_{SB}=R_{BS}^{\mathsf T}RSB​=RBST​.
  • Treat a known sensor tilt as a complete anatomical calibration.
  • Reuse an offset after the sensor moves relative to the segment.
  • Interpret agreement in one calibration pose as accuracy throughout motion.

Oral checks

  1. Describe the direction of RBSR_{BS}RBS​ without using the word “offset.”
  2. Why can gravity identify tilt but leave a heading ambiguity?
  3. Which assumption fails when an attachment slips during movement?

Continue to Orientation Estimation and Relative Joint Kinematics to separate estimation error from mounting error.

Check your understanding

  • Define the direction of every rotation matrix before composing transforms.
  • Recover segment orientation from a known rigid mounting offset.
  • Explain what static gravity can and cannot identify.
  • Distinguish sensor bias calibration, anatomical calibration and heading registration.
