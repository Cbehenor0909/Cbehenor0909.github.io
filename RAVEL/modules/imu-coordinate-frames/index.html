What does an IMU actually tell us?

Output What it represents What it does not directly provide
Accelerometer, m/s2\mathrm{m/s^2}m/s2 Specific force, resolved along the sensor axes Position, or movement acceleration with gravity already removed
Gyroscope, rad/s\mathrm{rad/s}rad/s or ∘/s{}^{\circ}\mathrm{/s}∘/s Angular velocity resolved about the sensor axes An absolute angle or a human joint angle
Magnetometer, often μT\mu\mathrm{T}μT Local magnetic field components; not simulated here A disturbance-free heading
Orientation, quaternion or matrix A derived estimate from an orientation algorithm; here the model supplies it exactly A direct raw reading from a single sensor channel

MEMS accelerometers use a proof mass; gyroscopes sense rotation through the Coriolis response. A supported sensor at rest has a nonzero accelerometer output. A freely falling ideal accelerometer reads zero. A magnetometer also responds to nearby magnetic sources. See VectorNav’s sensor explanation.

Orientation estimation adds assumptions: integrating angular rate needs an initial attitude and accumulates error; interpreting acceleration as a gravity direction fails during sufficiently strong motion. Static gravity constrains tilt but cannot determine yaw. See VectorNav’s AHRS explanation.

Three frames, one attachment

WWW — world: a fixed, right-handed, local frame with zzz pointing up. BBB — body segment: a teaching frame at the shoulder, with the neutral arm extending along −b^z-\hat{\mathbf{b}}_z−b^z​. SSS — sensor: axes fixed to the attached IMU. These labels identify where vector components are expressed; the geometry remains the same. Reference-frame background.

This module uses column vectors. RABR_{AB}RAB​ maps coordinates from BBB into AAA. Therefore:

vW=RWBRBSvS=RWSvS.\mathbf{v}_W = R_{WB}R_{BS}\mathbf{v}_S = R_{WS}\mathbf{v}_S.vW​=RWB​RBS​vS​=RWS​vS​.

The inverse mapping is the transpose of the rotation matrix. Our pose and mounting rotations both use Rz(yaw)Ry(pitch)Rx(roll)R_z(\mathrm{yaw})R_y(\mathrm{pitch})R_x(\mathrm{roll})Rz​(yaw)Ry​(pitch)Rx​(roll). These are illustrative rotation parameters, not anatomical shoulder angles. The sensor center is at rB=[0.035, 0, −d]\mathbf{r}_B = [0.035,\,0,\,-d]rB​=[0.035,0,−d] metres; changing the mounting angles rotates the sensor about that center.

RBSR_{BS}RBS​ stays constant while the rigid segment moves. In a real workflow, sensor-to-segment calibration must establish this relationship. For example, OpenSim IMU Placer uses calibration orientations and a matching model pose to determine offsets. Here you specify the offset exactly; no anatomical calibration is being estimated.

Try four experiments

  1. Rest / reset. Read [0, 0, 9.81] m/s2[0,\,0,\,9.81]\,\mathrm{m/s^2}[0,0,9.81]m/s2 and zero degrees per second. Change static segment pitch: acceleration components rotate but their norm remains 1g1g1g. Change yaw alone: static gravity cannot reveal that rotation.
  2. Rotate IMU 90∘90^{\circ}90∘. The arm stays still; mount roll becomes +90∘+90^{\circ}+90∘. The approximately +1g+1g+1g component moves from SzS_zSz​ to SyS_ySy​. Sensor coordinates changed; the physical specific-force vector did not.
  3. Swing the arm. Press Play, or scrub Model time. At the middle of the swing, angular speed is largest. At a reversal, angular speed is zero but angular acceleration is not. Move the sensor closer to the shoulder to reduce lever-arm acceleration; the gyroscope is unchanged.
  4. Translate only. The segment keeps its orientation while the shoulder moves vertically. The accelerometer varies and the ideal gyroscope stays zero. At the highest point, velocity is zero but downward acceleration is largest.

Pause holds a model instant for inspection. It does not replace that instant’s velocity with zero. Changing static pose sliders creates separate stationary snapshots; slider speed is not treated as physical motion.

From motion to readings

The prescribed trajectory is evaluated analytically. For a sensor rigidly attached at displacement rW\mathbf{r}_WrW​ from the shoulder, point acceleration contains translation, tangential, and centripetal terms:

aW=q¨W+αW×rW+ωW×(ωW×rW).\begin{aligned} \mathbf{a}_W &= \ddot{\mathbf{q}}_W + \boldsymbol{\alpha}_W\times\mathbf{r}_W \\ &\quad + \boldsymbol{\omega}_W\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}_W\times\mathbf{r}_W). \end{aligned}aW​​=q¨​W​+αW​×rW​+ωW​×(ωW​×rW​).​

This is the rigid-body point-acceleration relation; see MIT’s kinematics derivation, pages 1–2. Here the oscillation has a fixed rotation axis and a fixed mounting offset, so there is no relative sensor motion term.

Subtract world gravity to obtain specific force, then express it in SSS. Express angular velocity in SSS with the same transpose:

fS=RWST(aW−gW),ωS=RWSTωW.\begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_S &= R_{WS}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{a}_W - \mathbf{g}_W), \\ \boldsymbol{\omega}_S &= R_{WS}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_W. \end{aligned}fS​ωS​​=RWST​(aW​−gW​),=RWST​ωW​.​

All calculations use metres, seconds and radians; gyroscope display values are converted to degrees per second. The expandable calculation panel shows each current contribution and all three matrices.

Scope and next steps

Every value here comes from an ideal synthetic rigid-body model. The upper arm is illustrative: there are no joint constraints, muscle dynamics, skin motion, sensor noise, bias, saturation, timing errors, magnetometer, orientation filter or live connection. World axes and gravity sign are explicit teaching conventions; device APIs may use different conventions or already corrected outputs.

Next lessons can separate raw signals from orientation estimation, demonstrate sensor-to-segment calibration, and compare two segments. Real-device integration will need declared axes, units, timestamps and output provenance before these formulas can be applied reliably.

Check your understanding

  • Explain why a supported, stationary accelerometer reads about 1 g while the gyroscope reads zero.
  • Use R_WS = R_WB R_BS to separate segment orientation from sensor mounting.
  • Predict which components change after a 90-degree mounting rotation.
  • Distinguish angular velocity, translational acceleration, and an orientation estimate.
  • Explain why sensor distance from the joint affects rotational acceleration but not rigid-body angular velocity.
