How do algebraic properties of a matrix explain the motion of a differential equation?

Read a matrix as a map

A matrix A∈Rn×nA\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}A∈Rn×n maps a state vector to another vector. In x˙=Ax\dot{\mathbf{x}}=A\mathbf{x}x˙=Ax, that new vector is the instantaneous direction and speed of the state trajectory. A matrix entry is not itself a system mode: the coupled equations must be considered together.

The column space contains all vectors AxA\mathbf{x}Ax can produce. The null space contains the vectors mapped to zero. Rank counts independent columns, and rank–nullity gives

rank⁡(A)+dim⁡ker⁡(A)=n.\operatorname{rank}(A)+\dim\ker(A)=n.rank(A)+dimker(A)=n.

An equilibrium of the unforced system satisfies Axe=0A\mathbf{x}_e=0Axe​=0. Full rank therefore makes the origin the unique equilibrium. Rank matters again when asking whether actuator directions span the state space in controllability and observability.

An eigenvector identifies a direction that the map preserves:

Av=λv.A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}.Av=λv.

Starting along that direction gives x(t)=ceλtv\mathbf{x}(t)=c e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}x(t)=ceλtv. Negative real part produces decay; positive real part produces growth. A complex conjugate pair produces real oscillatory trajectories with an exponential envelope. Eigenvectors describe directions in state coordinates, not necessarily physical displacement directions.

From modes to a general solution

For a constant matrix, the matrix exponential solves the homogeneous initial-value problem. With a forcing input,

x˙=Ax+Bu,x(t)=eAtx(0)+∫0teA(t−τ)Bu(τ) dτ.\begin{aligned} \dot{\mathbf{x}}&=A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{u},\\ \mathbf{x}(t)&=e^{At}\mathbf{x}(0) +\int_0^t e^{A(t-\tau)}B\mathbf{u}(\tau)\,d\tau. \end{aligned}x˙x(t)​=Ax+Bu,=eAtx(0)+∫0t​eA(t−τ)Bu(τ)dτ.​

The first term carries the initial condition; the second accumulates input effects. These equations assume a finite-dimensional, continuous-time linear time-invariant model. With a time-varying A(t)A(t)A(t), replace eA(t−τ)e^{A(t-\tau)}eA(t−τ) by the appropriate state-transition matrix.

If A=VΛV−1A=V\Lambda V^{-1}A=VΛV−1, then eAt=VeΛtV−1e^{At}=V e^{\Lambda t}V^{-1}eAt=VeΛtV−1. Diagonalization is useful because scalar exponentials evolve independently in modal coordinates. It requires a complete set of independent eigenvectors. The author’s chapter on linear systems gives a broader treatment of this solution structure.

Read the diagram. This is a plane of states, not the mass’s physical track: horizontal distance represents displacement, and vertical distance represents velocity. The two blue lines are preserved modal directions. Motion starting on either line shrinks toward the origin; the second mode shrinks faster. The repeated-eigenvalue trap

Repeated eigenvalues alone do not establish diagonalizability. For

A=[0100],eAt=[1t01],A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad e^{At}=\begin{bmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{bmatrix},A=[00​10​],eAt=[10​t1​],

both eigenvalues are zero, yet an initial velocity causes unbounded position. A Jordan block contributes polynomial factors multiplying exponentials. For continuous-time LTI systems, asymptotic stability requires every eigenvalue strictly in the left half-plane. Lyapunov stability permits imaginary-axis eigenvalues only when their Jordan blocks are size one.

Oral practice

  • Does a negative trace prove stability? No. It constrains the sum of eigenvalues; one eigenvalue can still be positive. For a real two-state system, negative trace and positive determinant together imply a Hurwitz matrix.
  • Why retain the initial-condition term? A transfer function describes zero-initial-state input/output behavior; it does not remove stored energy in the physical system.
  • Can changing coordinates change stability? An invertible similarity transformation preserves eigenvalues. It changes how the same motion is described.

Continue to state-space modeling to construct A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D from mechanics, then to Laplace transforms to solve the same equations in the complex domain.

Check your understanding

  • Distinguish eigenvalues, eigenvectors, rank and null spaces.
  • Separate initial-condition response from forced response.
  • Solve a two-state mechanical example by modal decomposition.
  • Recognize why repeated eigenvalues require checking Jordan structure.
