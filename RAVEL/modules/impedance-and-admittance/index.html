Impedance and Admittance · Designing Interaction

Design the relationship between contact force and motion, distinguish force-command and motion-command implementations, and use energy to explain an ideal passive interaction.

advancedFunctionalReading lesson

Should a contact force create a motion command, or should a motion deviation create a force command?

Contact couples force and motion

A tracking controller tries to reduce position error. During contact, rigidly insisting on a position can create large forces. Interaction control instead asks how the robot should move when the environment pushes, and how strongly it should resist displacement. The sensor estimate, actuator interface and mechanical compliance all affect the achievable behavior.

Read the diagram. The top row produces a force or torque command from motion deviation. The bottom row uses measured force to generate a motion reference for an inner servo. Both can aim for the same mass-spring-damper behavior, but the signal direction and actuator assumptions differ. Arrows show information flow, not guaranteed hardware bandwidth.

Two causal implementations

For zero initial conditions, mechanical impedance relates force to velocity:

An impedance implementation measures motion and commands effort through a suitable torque/force interface. An admittance implementation measures interaction force, integrates the virtual dynamic equation to generate xc,x˙cx_c,\dot x_cxc​,x˙c​, and asks a motion servo to track them. The inner servo’s dynamics remain part of the combined system. A force sensor does not by itself establish that the force is unbiased or expressed in the correct contact frame.

These names describe an input/output relationship at an interaction port. They are not interchangeable software blocks independently of the robot’s hardware. See Hogan’s interaction-control notes.

Energy explains the ideal passive behavior

For this fixed-reference continuous model, stored energy is

E=12Mde˙2+12Kde2,E˙=Fexte˙−Dde˙2.E=\frac12M_d\dot e^2+\frac12K_d e^2, \qquad \dot E=F_{\mathrm{ext}}\dot e-D_d\dot e^2.E=21​Md​e˙2+21​Kd​e2,E˙=Fext​e˙−Dd​e˙2.

The product Fexte˙F_{\mathrm{ext}}\dot eFext​e˙ is power entering the robot; damping removes nonnegative power. The ideal system is passive at this port. Passivity alone does not mean zero contact force, and stability of an interconnection still requires the relevant interconnection and dissipation conditions. Moving the reference, varying stiffness, sampling, delay, saturation and imperfect inner servos can change the energy balance. Positive requested gains alone do not prove the implemented interaction passive.

Read the paper figure. Follow motion error, external force and the adjustable impedance parameters through the loop. The source diagram assumes a stationary desired position: desired velocity and acceleration are zero. Its adaptation block changes the requested mechanical behavior. Time-varying gains require an energy/stability analysis beyond the fixed-parameter example above; an impedance label alone establishes no passivity guarantee.

The review distinguishes control, learning and their combination. Hogan (2022) offers an advanced review of contact and physical interaction. Follow the paper guide for a concrete sensing-to-admittance architecture.

Oral checks and common errors

  • What does admittance integrate? A force-driven virtual dynamic model whose resulting motion becomes a servo reference.
  • Why can a stationary contact have nonzero force? The virtual spring balances the load at a nonzero displacement.
  • Why does a moving reference matter? It can supply energy; the fixed-reference passivity calculation no longer contains all input power.

Common errors: defining impedance as force divided by position, reversing the contact-force sign, or claiming a simulation establishes human-robot interaction performance. Connect this lesson to Sampled Feedback and the synthetic sensing-to-control experiment before reasoning about a complete loop.

Check your understanding

  • Explain impedance and admittance using effort and motion at a contact port.
  • Define consistent force, displacement error and velocity signs.
  • Calculate the response of a desired mass spring damper interaction.
  • Bound the passivity claim to a fixed-reference ideal model.
