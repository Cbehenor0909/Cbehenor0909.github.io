Feedback Interconnections · Blocks and Signal-Flow Graphs

Derive interconnected transfer functions from signal equations, then use Mason's rule while keeping feedback signs and non-touching loops explicit.

intermediateFunctionalReading lesson

How can several paths and feedback loops be reduced without losing their signs or interactions?

Write signals before reducing blocks

A block diagram encodes linear relations between signals. A summing junction defines an algebraic equation, and a takeoff copies a signal. The safest reduction begins by naming these signals; the sign at a feedback junction must survive every manipulation.

Read the diagram. Follow the blue arrows once around the loop. The returned signal HyHyHy is subtracted at the junction, so the block receives the error e=r−Hye=r-Hye=r−Hy. The output takeoff copies yyy; it does not perform another subtraction. Keeping the minus sign at that one junction explains the plus sign that appears when feedback is moved to the denominator.

For independent SISO blocks with no loading interaction, series gains multiply and parallel gains add. For negative feedback,

e=r−Hy,y=Ge⟹YR=G1+GH.e=r-Hy,\qquad y=Ge \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{Y}{R}=\frac{G}{1+GH}.e=r−Hy,y=Ge⟹RY​=1+GHG​.

Positive feedback instead gives G/(1−GH)G/(1-GH)G/(1−GH). These statements assume the interconnection has a unique causal solution. If blocks have direct feedthrough, an algebraic loop may require an invertible instantaneous coefficient; a zero denominator there signals an ill-posed interconnection. Matrix-valued block multiplication also depends on order.

The MIT block-diagram lecture develops the basic series, parallel and feedback rules.

Worked example: two independently regulated paths

Let each branch receive the same reference and have its own local negative feedback:

z1=G1(r−H1z1),z2=G2(r−H2z2),y=z1+z2.\begin{aligned} z_1&=G_1(r-H_1z_1),\\ z_2&=G_2(r-H_2z_2),\\ y&=z_1+z_2. \end{aligned}z1​z2​y​=G1​(r−H1​z1​),=G2​(r−H2​z2​),=z1​+z2​.​

Solving the branches first gives

YR=G11+G1H1+G21+G2H2.\frac{Y}{R} =\frac{G_1}{1+G_1H_1}+\frac{G_2}{1+G_2H_2}.RY​=1+G1​H1​G1​​+1+G2​H2​G2​​.

For G1=1/(s+1)G_1=1/(s+1)G1​=1/(s+1), G2=2/(s+2)G_2=2/(s+2)G2​=2/(s+2) and H1=H2=1H_1=H_2=1H1​=H2​=1,

YR=1s+2+2s+4=3s+8s2+6s+8.\frac{Y}{R} =\frac{1}{s+2}+\frac{2}{s+4} =\frac{3s+8}{s^2+6s+8}.RY​=s+21​+s+42​=s2+6s+83s+8​.

The DC gain is one, and the branch poles are −2-2−2 and −4-4−4. This topology differs from placing one feedback loop around the summed open-loop branches, which gives (G1+G2)/[1+H(G1+G2)](G_1+G_2)/[1+H(G_1+G_2)](G1​+G2​)/[1+H(G1​+G2​)]. Feedback location matters even when the same block names appear.

Mason’s rule reproduces the algebra

A signal-flow graph uses nodes for variables and directed branches for signed gains. For the example, the graph can be specified without ambiguity:

Read the diagram. The forward paths share the reference and output. The loops do not: the upper loop visits only z1z_1z1​, and the lower loop visits only z2z_2z2​. They are therefore non-touching and their product belongs in Mason’s determinant. The negative signs are already part of the loop gains; subtracting a negative loop gain then produces the positive feedback factors in the denominator.

A forward path reaches the output without repeating a node. A loop returns to its starting node without repeating another node. A path or loop gain is the product of its branch gains. Two loops are non-touching only when they share no nodes; proximity on the drawing does not matter.

Mason’s gain formula is

YR=∑kPkΔkΔ,Δ=1−∑iLi+∑non-touching pairsLiLj−∑pairwise non-touching triplesLiLjLℓ+⋯ .\begin{aligned} \frac{Y}{R}&=\frac{\sum_k P_k\Delta_k}{\Delta},\\ \Delta&=1-\sum_i L_i +\sum_{\text{non-touching pairs}}L_iL_j\\ &\quad-\sum_{\text{pairwise non-touching triples}}L_iL_jL_\ell+\cdots. \end{aligned}RY​Δ​=Δ∑k​Pk​Δk​​,=1−i∑​Li​+non-touching pairs∑​Li​Lj​−pairwise non-touching triples∑​Li​Lj​Lℓ​+⋯.​

The cofactor Δk\Delta_kΔk​ is formed after removing every loop that touches forward path kkk. This graph interpretation is associated with Mason’s primary report, cataloged at MIT.

Here P1=G1P_1=G_1P1​=G1​, P2=G2P_2=G_2P2​=G2​, L1=−G1H1L_1=-G_1H_1L1​=−G1​H1​ and L2=−G2H2L_2=-G_2H_2L2​=−G2​H2​. The two self-loops occupy distinct nodes, so

Δ=1−L1−L2+L1L2=(1+G1H1)(1+G2H2),Δ1=1+G2H2,Δ2=1+G1H1.\begin{aligned} \Delta&=1-L_1-L_2+L_1L_2\\ &=(1+G_1H_1)(1+G_2H_2),\\ \Delta_1&=1+G_2H_2,\qquad \Delta_2=1+G_1H_1. \end{aligned}ΔΔ1​​=1−L1​−L2​+L1​L2​=(1+G1​H1​)(1+G2​H2​),=1+G2​H2​,Δ2​=1+G1​H1​.​

Substitution produces exactly the branch-sum result. Dropping L1L2L_1L_2L1​L2​ would incorrectly remove the interaction needed for the common denominator.

Common errors and a stability boundary

Moving a summing junction across a block generally requires changing branch gains. Moving a takeoff also changes which signal is copied. Writing the original node equations and checking the reduced equations is more reliable than memorizing drawing tricks.

A simplified Y/RY/RY/R does not reveal every internal signal or unstable canceled mode. Inspect transfers from disturbances injected at internal junctions and the full interconnected state model before claiming internal stability. The output map and the system’s hidden memory answer different questions.

Oral practice

  • Why does negative feedback produce a plus in the denominator? Rearranging y=G(r−Hy)y=G(r-Hy)y=G(r−Hy) moves GHyGHyGHy to the left side.
  • Do the two example loops touch at the common reference? No. Neither loop traverses rrr or yyy; their nodes are z1z_1z1​ and z2z_2z2​ respectively.
  • When is Δk=1\Delta_k=1Δk​=1? When no loops remain after removing those touching path kkk.

Continue with stability and performance to evaluate what the interconnected poles and sensitivity functions imply.

Check your understanding

  • Derive series, parallel and feedback formulas from signal equations.
  • Apply Mason's determinant and forward-path cofactors.
  • Identify non-touching loops by shared nodes.
  • Check interconnection well-posedness and internal stability separately.
